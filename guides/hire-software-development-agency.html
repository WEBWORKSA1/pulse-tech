How to Hire a Software Development Agency: A Buyer's Checklist

By Pulse.Tech Editorial · 9 min read · Updated November 2026

How to scope a project, compare agencies, read proposals and structure contracts so your app ships on time and on budget.

Hiring an agency to build a website, app or internal tool is one of the highest-stakes purchases a growing business makes. Projects fail less from bad code than from vague scope, mismatched expectations and weak contracts. Use this checklist.

Before you contact anyone

  • Write a one-page brief: the problem, the users, the must-have features, the deadline and the budget range.
  • Separate must-haves from nice-to-haves. Your first release should be the smallest version that delivers value.
  • Decide who owns the project internally and how many hours a week they can give.

Shortlisting agencies

Look for three to five firms with shipped work similar to yours in industry or technology. Ask for two client references from projects finished in the last 18 months and actually call them. Ask the references what went wrong and how the agency handled it.

Reading proposals

  • Discovery phase: good agencies propose a short paid discovery to refine scope before quoting the full build.
  • Assumptions: the proposal should list them explicitly. Hidden assumptions become change requests.
  • Team: who will actually work on your project, and what share of their time.
  • Process: sprint length, demo cadence, how you approve work.

Pricing models

Fixed price works for tightly defined scope. Time and materials fits evolving products but needs a budget cap and weekly reporting. Dedicated team suits long-running product work. Many successful projects use fixed-price discovery followed by capped time and materials.

Contract essentials

  1. You own the code and intellectual property on payment.
  2. Code lives in a repository you control from day one.
  3. Milestone-based payments tied to accepted deliverables.
  4. A warranty period for bug fixes after launch.
  5. Clear handover: documentation, credentials, deployment steps.

Red flags

  • A quote without questions.
  • No access to the code until the end.
  • Pressure to sign quickly with a discount.
